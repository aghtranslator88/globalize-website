## Choose the right level of human review

The review required depends on the material and its intended use. A draft for internal orientation may need a different review depth from a public-facing page, a contract, a medical record, a financial statement, a safety instruction, or an official document. Describe the consequences of an error, the intended reader, and the parts that require particular attention. The project scope should state whether the output is a translation, a summary, a rewrite, or a combination; these tasks are not interchangeable.

Human review should compare the output with the source, not merely make the new text sound fluent. Check names, numbers, dates, negation, qualifications, references, terminology, completeness, and whether the language model added or omitted meaning. For specialist material, the reviewer should have the relevant language and subject competence for the assigned task. A fluent result is not by itself evidence that a claim or figure is correct.

| Check | Review question |
|---|---|
| Meaning | Does each sentence preserve the source’s statement and limits? |
| Completeness | Are headings, notes, tables, labels, and footnotes accounted for? |
| Data | Do names, dates, amounts, identifiers, and units match the source? |
| Unsupported output | Did the system invent a fact, citation, or explanation? |
| Intended use | Is the review level appropriate for the audience and consequence? |

## Set data and workflow boundaries before using tools

Before sending material to any automated system, confirm what content may be processed, which tool is authorized, and whether the file contains confidential, personal, regulated, or commercially sensitive information. Do not place passwords, access tokens, or unnecessary personal data in prompts. The project owner should follow the applicable contract, privacy policy, and information-handling rules for the actual tool; this page does not promise a particular retention or security control.

Keep the source, generated output, reviewer edits, and final approved version distinguishable. Record the tool and version when the organization’s workflow requires it, and make corrections traceable for high-impact material. If the source is unclear, the correct next step is clarification from its owner, not asking a model to guess. Where a recipient requires a named certification, authorized translator, or specific process, verify that requirement separately before choosing a workflow.

AI assistance can support suitable language tasks, but it does not replace professional judgment, subject review, the source owner’s decisions, or the receiving authority’s rules. Confirm the deliverable and review boundary before work begins.

## Define approval and correction ownership

A project brief should identify who may accept machine-assisted language, who performs the human review, and who approves the final output. Decide how corrections will be returned and whether a change to the source requires a new comparison. Keep a clean final copy separate from working prompts and intermediate drafts. Do not treat a model’s self-assessment, confidence statement, or fluent wording as an independent quality certificate. The responsible reviewer should be able to trace important edits to the source and explain why they were made.
