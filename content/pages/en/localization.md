## What localization means for a digital product

Localization adapts content and resources for a particular language, region, or audience. It may involve more than replacing words, but the exact work depends on the product and the agreed scope. W3C describes internationalization as designing and developing so a product can be adapted across languages and cultures; Microsoft’s application guidance discusses adapting resources for particular languages, regions, and markets. These general explanations do not define Globalize’s deliverables.

## Map the content before estimating the work

A localization enquiry is easier to scope when the source material and its dependencies are visible. List the content areas and identify which version is current. Separate text intended for customers from internal notes, technical references, and content that must remain unchanged. Note where a string appears, who sees it, and what action it supports; a short label can have a different meaning in a button, error message, or help page.

## Prepare a localization project brief

Begin by identifying the content and product areas in scope: public web pages, app screens, help material, software messages, marketing copy, or another defined set. State the source and target locales, audience, purpose, release date, and whether the work is a one-time launch or recurring updates. A language name alone does not specify regional spelling, currency, date format, tone, or whether a particular dialect or register is required.

Provide source files in their original editable format when available, along with screenshots, context notes, character limits, variable definitions, and a list of strings that must remain unchanged. Mark placeholders and code so they are not treated as translatable prose. Identify whether the content is stored in a repository, design file, spreadsheet, content-management system, or another tool; the requested handoff should match the actual workflow rather than an assumed platform integration.

| Input | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Target locale and audience | Determines regional conventions and vocabulary choices |
| Screenshots or context | Shows where a string appears and what it refers to |
| Variables and placeholders | Prevents names, values, or code tokens from being altered |
| Glossary and style guide | Keeps product terminology and tone consistent |
| File format and workflow | Defines what can be reviewed and how the result is returned |

## Separate language review from product testing

A linguistic review checks meaning, grammar, terminology, consistency, and fit for the intended audience. It does not by itself prove that the translated product works correctly, that a button performs the intended action, that a layout fits every screen, or that the product complies with local law. If functional testing, engineering changes, legal review, or publication support is needed, specify those tasks and confirm their availability separately.

For right-to-left content, identify the relevant screens and mixed-direction strings, including numbers, email addresses, links, and embedded English terms. A language reviewer can flag a directional or spacing problem visible in the supplied context, but final rendering must be checked in the actual product or preview environment. Provide the owner and test build if visual validation is included in the agreed scope.

For ongoing releases, keep a versioned list of changed strings and specify how obsolete text is handled. Agree who approves terminology and how late source edits are reported. Before launch, the product owner should compare the final integrated version with the approved source and test the specific screens affected by the release. Localization improves language and cultural fit; it does not guarantee market performance or legal compliance.
